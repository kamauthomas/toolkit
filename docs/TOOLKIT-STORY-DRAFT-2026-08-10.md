# More Than Training: Why The Toolkit Exists

Status: editorial draft for review; not published

There is a question at the heart of The Toolkit for Skills and Innovation: what
happens when a young person has ability, but lacks the training, certification
or industry connection needed to turn that ability into decent work?

It is the question behind an official Toolkit video titled *TTI—Why We Exist*,
featuring founder and director Jane Muigai Kamphuis. It is also the thread that
connects the organization’s work across technical trades, renewable energy,
digital skills and pathways into employment or entrepreneurship.

## A response shaped by experience

The Toolkit describes itself as a Kenya-based social enterprise founded in
2014 to disrupt youth unemployment. Its public mission is to transform
vulnerable youth to prosperity through innovation and skills for current and
future labour markets.

Jane’s published biography gives that mission a personal history. Before
founding Toolkit, she worked with the United Nations Refugee Agency in conflict
settings. The biography says those experiences showed her how unemployment and
exclusion can leave young people vulnerable to violence, crime, drugs,
radicalisation and irregular migration. She founded Toolkit in response to that
risk and to a shortage of skilled labour in growing sectors.

The answer was not training for its own sake. It was a route from potential to
participation.

## Skills connected to a destination

Toolkit’s model brings together several parts of that route. Learners build life
and employability skills, develop competence in a technical trade, encounter
modern tools and workplace practice, and move toward certification, employment
or enterprise.

A 2020 MIT Solve profile described this as a three-pillar approach: life and
employability preparation; technical learning that blends class and workplace
experience; and industry linkage through workplace-based training. That profile
is historical, but the underlying idea remains visible in Toolkit’s current
public description of its work: skills should respond to labour-market demand,
and collaboration with regulators, development partners and employers should
help learners convert training into opportunity.

This distinction matters. A course can end when a learner completes a module.
A pathway continues until skill can be demonstrated, recognised and used.

## Innovation with a practical purpose

Toolkit’s programmes have applied that pathway in fields including welding,
solar technology, digital work and entrepreneurship. Innovation is useful here
because it changes how learners practise, how quickly trainers can identify
gaps and how safely technical competence can be developed.

In April 2023, Don Bosco Tech Africa reported the launch of Toolkit’s advanced
MIG welding programme, combining virtual-reality preparation with physical
workshop training. The technology was not presented as a substitute for
hands-on work. It was part of a sequence designed to prepare learners for the
workshop with greater familiarity and confidence.

Renewable energy provides another example. Toolkit’s programme material
describes training and certification pathways intended to connect young people
to opportunities in the solar value chain. Its published projects have also
placed particular emphasis on women and learners from marginalised communities.

These examples point to the same principle: innovation earns its place when it
helps someone learn more effectively, work more safely or reach an opportunity
that was previously out of view.

## Partnership as part of the infrastructure

No training provider creates an employment pathway alone. Employers understand
changing skill demand. Regulatory and certification bodies make competence
recognisable. Development partners can help programmes reach communities that
the market routinely overlooks.

A 2021 Don Bosco Tech Africa report recorded a partnership with Toolkit focused
on advanced training, certification and employment or self-employment in solar
technology, welding and scaffolding. Toolkit’s current institutional summary
likewise describes collaboration with public bodies, private companies and
development partners as central to its interventions.

The value of those relationships is not the length of a partner list. It is
whether they reduce the distance between learning and work.

## Measuring the story carefully

Toolkit’s public figures show growth, but they also illustrate why impact must
be reported with dates and context. A January 2024 Toolkit feature reported
8,017 youth and women empowered. The current Toolkit website displays a total of
11,190 youth and women impacted, but the page does not state an “as of” date.

For that reason, the larger number should be treated as a currently displayed
institutional figure, not as an independently audited headline. Employment
rates, income changes, completed project outcomes and current geographic reach
should be supported by a dated internal impact report before publication.

Accuracy strengthens the story. It allows the experience of learners to carry
the meaning, while numbers provide accountable evidence rather than decoration.

## Why Toolkit exists

The Toolkit story begins with a belief that talent should not be lost because a
young person entered the labour market without the right credential, equipment
or connection. Its work seeks to make that talent visible: through practical
skills, confidence, certification and a clearer route to work or enterprise.

That is why the organization’s story is larger than a catalogue of courses. It
is a story about building a bridge—between informal ability and recognised
competence, between training and industry, and between a young person’s
potential and the chance to use it.
